Em dashes get all the attention, but passive voice is the quieter AI tell. Generated drafts lean on it constantly: things "are considered", results "were observed", decisions "have been made" - by no one in particular. The effect is text that sounds authoritative while saying very little. The good news is that passive voice is mechanical enough to spot reliably, and once you see it, you can decide sentence by sentence whether to keep it or rewrite it.

## What is passive voice?

In an active sentence, the subject does the action: "The team shipped the feature." In a passive sentence, the subject receives the action and the doer is demoted or dropped entirely: "The feature was shipped (by the team)." The grammatical fingerprint is a form of *to be* (`is`, `are`, `was`, `were`, `been`, `being`) plus a past participle (`shipped`, `written`, `considered`, `made`). When the actor disappears - "mistakes were made" - that's passive voice doing exactly what it's good at: hiding responsibility.

## How do I find passive voice in my writing?

Three approaches, fastest to slowest:

1. **Paste it into a highlighter.** The quickest route is to drop your text into our [passive-voice and weasel-word highlighter](/tools/passive-voice-weasel-word-highlighter), which marks every *be*-verb-plus-participle construction so you can scan the whole piece in seconds instead of reading it three times.
2. **Search for the trigger words.** Use find (Ctrl/Cmd-F) for `was`, `were`, `is`, `are`, `been`, and `by`. Not every hit is passive, but every passive sentence contains one, so it narrows the field.
3. **Read it aloud and ask "who is doing this?"** If a sentence describes an action but never names the actor, you're probably looking at passive voice.

The highlighter approach wins for anything longer than a paragraph because passive constructions are easy to read past - your brain fills in the meaning and skips the structure.

## How do I check for passive voice in Word?

Microsoft Word has a built-in check, but it's off by default. Go to **File - Options - Proofing**, click **Settings** next to "Writing Style", scroll to the **Style** section, and tick **Passive Voice**. From then on, Editor flags passive sentences with a blue underline. The catch: Word checks one document at a time, misses plenty of constructions, and says nothing about weasel words or weak adverbs. ## What are weasel words?

Weasel words are vague qualifiers that imply a claim without committing to one. Classics: *some*, *many*, *most*, *several*, *experts say*, *studies show*, *it is widely believed*, *arguably*, *relatively*, *fairly*. They sound substantive but evaporate under scrutiny - "studies show" which studies? "Many users prefer" how many? AI writing is full of them because they let a model sound confident about facts it doesn't actually have. Spotting weasel words is the natural companion to passive voice, which is why our highlighter flags both in the same pass.

## What are weak adverbs, and should I remove them?

Weak adverbs are the `-ly` words that prop up a verb that should stand on its own: *very*, *really*, *quite*, *actually*, *basically*, *clearly*, *simply*. "She ran very quickly" is weaker than "She sprinted." Not every adverb is bad - "he spoke quietly" carries real information - but a draft stuffed with intensifiers usually has stronger verbs hiding underneath. The rule of thumb: if deleting the adverb doesn't change the meaning, delete it. If a stronger verb makes the adverb redundant, swap it in.

## Why is passive voice bad - and is it always wrong?

It isn't always wrong, and anyone who tells you to eliminate every passive sentence is overselling the rule. Passive voice is the right choice when:

- **The actor is unknown or irrelevant:** "The bridge was built in 1890."
- **The receiver is the real subject:** "The patient was treated within an hour."
- **You're being tactful on purpose:** "The deadline was missed" softens blame when blame isn't the point.

Passive voice becomes a problem when it's the *default* rather than a deliberate choice. It adds words, buries the actor, and flattens rhythm - all of which make prose feel generic and machine-made. The goal isn't zero passive sentences; it's passive sentences you chose on purpose. A highlighter helps because it surfaces every instance so you can make that call consciously instead of letting it pile up unnoticed.

## How do I fix passive voice and change it to active voice?

The mechanical fix is a three-step swap:

1. **Find the real actor** - the "by ___" phrase, stated or implied. ("The report was reviewed *by the editor*.")
2. **Move it to the front** as the subject. ("The editor...")
3. **Turn the participle back into a plain verb.** ("...reviewed the report.")

"A decision was made by the committee" becomes "The committee decided." Shorter, clearer, and you know who did it. If there's genuinely no actor worth naming, that's your signal the passive might be fine to keep - or that the sentence needs a real subject you've been avoiding.

## How can I make my writing more concise?

Passive voice, weasel words, and weak adverbs are the three biggest sources of padding, and they tend to travel together. Clear them in one editing pass:

- Convert default passives to active.
- Cut weasel words or replace them with a specific claim.
- Delete intensifiers that don't change the meaning.

These are the same kinds of edits that make AI-assisted drafts read like a human wrote them - and they pair naturally with the punctuation and vocabulary fixes covered in our guide to the [signs of AI writing](/blog/signs-of-ai-writing).
