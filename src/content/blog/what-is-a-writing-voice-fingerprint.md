You can usually tell a text from a friend before you reach their name. The rhythm gives it away - short jabs, a favourite filler word, the way they never use a semicolon. That recognisable pattern is your **writing voice fingerprint**: the measurable habits that make your prose sound like you and nobody else. This post explains what a voice fingerprint is, how to build a profile from your own samples, and how to catch the moment a draft - especially an AI-assisted one - stops sounding like you.

## What is a writing voice profile?

A writing voice profile is a snapshot of your stylistic habits, captured from text you've already written. It's not a grade and it's not a style guide someone handed you. It's a description of *how you actually write*: your typical sentence length, how often you use contractions, your favourite transition words, whether you lean on dashes or commas, how formal you tend to be, and the vocabulary you reach for without thinking.

Think of it as a fingerprint because no single feature defines you - it's the combination. Lots of people write short sentences. Lots use "honestly" as an opener. The specific *mix* of those traits, in your proportions, is what's distinctive. Once you've measured that mix, you can compare any new draft against it and see where the two diverge.

## What makes writing sound like you?

A handful of features carry most of the signal:

- **Sentence-length rhythm.** Bursty writing (a three-word sentence next to a thirty-word one) reads very differently from steady, uniform prose. Your typical spread is a strong tell.
- **Function words.** The small, "invisible" words - *and, but, just, so, really, that* - are used so unconsciously that they're hard to fake. Forensic linguists rely on them precisely because writers don't control them on purpose.
- **Punctuation habits.** Whether you favour the em dash, the semicolon, the comma splice, or the full stop is remarkably consistent per person.
- **Contractions and formality.** "Do not" versus "don't" across a document tells you a lot about register.
- **Signature vocabulary.** Not buzzwords - the plain words you happen to over-use.

None of these are about correctness. They're about identity. That's why the same features that flag [common signs of AI writing](/blog/signs-of-ai-writing) also flag *anyone* writing outside their normal range.

## How do I create a writing voice profile?

The practical steps:

1. **Gather real samples.** Collect three to five pieces you wrote yourself, unedited by anyone else - emails, blog posts, notes. The more natural and recent, the better.
2. **Measure the features.** Run them through a tool that calculates the stats above, or build a profile by hand if you enjoy spreadsheets. Our [voice and style memory tool](/tools/local-voice-style-memory) does this in your browser and saves the result locally.
3. **Save it.** A profile is only useful if you can reuse it. Store it so the next draft you write can be checked against the same baseline.
4. **Compare new drafts against it.** When you finish something - or when AI finishes something for you - run the comparison and look at where the new text falls outside your usual range.

## How many writing samples do I need to build a voice profile?

More is better, but you hit useful accuracy faster than people expect. Two or three substantial samples (a few hundred words each) are enough to estimate sentence rhythm and punctuation habits. Five to ten gives you a stable read on vocabulary and function-word frequency. Beyond that you're refining, not transforming, the profile.

A few rules of thumb: use samples from the *same kind* of writing you want to check (don't profile your casual texts and then judge a formal report against them), and avoid pieces a heavy editor already reshaped - you'd be profiling the editor, not yourself.

## Why does my draft not sound like me anymore?

Almost always, it's one of three things:

- **An AI tool rewrote more than you asked.** You requested a tidy-up; it also smoothed your rhythm, swapped your plain words for "leverage" and "robust", and dropped in some em dashes. Death by a thousand polish passes.
- **You wrote outside your normal context.** Tired, rushed, or imitating a format you don't normally use.
- **Multiple hands touched it.** Co-authors and editors each leave a fingerprint, and the result reads like a committee.

The fix isn't to distrust editing - it's to make the drift *visible* so you can decide, sentence by sentence, what to keep.

## What is voice drift, and how do I find off-voice sentences?

**Voice drift** is the gradual divergence between a draft and your established voice, usually accumulating one small edit at a time. It's easy to miss because each individual change looks reasonable. The cumulative effect is a document that's grammatically fine and completely impersonal.

To find off-voice sentences, compare the draft against your profile and look for outliers: a sentence three times longer than anything you normally write, a sudden cluster of formal connectors ("furthermore", "moreover"), contractions vanishing halfway through, or vocabulary you'd never use. ## How do I tell if an AI editor changed my writing voice?

Run a before-and-after. Profile your original draft, accept the AI edits, then compare the edited version against your profile. The features that shifted most are exactly where the tool overrode you. Common offenders: a drop in sentence-length variety (AI loves uniform, medium-length sentences), a spike in em dashes, fewer contractions, and the appearance of cliché connective tissue. You can then re-introduce the human edits selectively rather than reverting everything - keep the genuine fixes, undo the personality erasure.

## A note on intent: this is about consistency, not impersonation

There's a version of "make AI sound like me" that drifts into trying to fake authorship or dodge detection. That's not what a voice fingerprint is for.
