"Title case" sounds like one rule, but ask two editors to capitalize the same headline and you'll often get two different answers. That's because the two most common American style guides - the Associated Press (AP) Stylebook and the Chicago Manual of Style - draw the line in different places. This post explains what title case actually is, where AP and Chicago disagree, and how to pick the right one without memorizing a rulebook.

## What is title case?

Title case is a capitalization style for headings, titles, and headlines where you capitalize the **important** words and leave the small connecting words lowercase. So "the lord of the rings" becomes "The Lord of the Rings" - *the*, *of*, and *the* stay lowercase because they're minor words, while *Lord* and *Rings* get capitals.

The catch is the definition of "important." Every style guide agrees on the big categories - nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs are always capitalized. The disagreements are all about the little words: articles (*a, an, the*), short conjunctions (*and, but, or*), and prepositions (*of, in, on, to, with*).

## What is the difference between title case and sentence case?

These two are easy to mix up:

- **Title case** capitalizes the important words: "How to Bake Sourdough Bread at Home."
- **Sentence case** capitalizes only the first word and any proper nouns, like a normal sentence: "How to bake sourdough bread at home."

Sentence case is increasingly popular for web headlines, UI labels, and email subject lines because it reads as friendlier and is easier to apply consistently (there's nothing to debate - just capitalize the first word). Title case still rules print headlines, book and article titles, and most formal publishing. ## How to capitalize a title in AP style

AP style is built for newspapers and most newsrooms and marketing teams. The rules:

- **Capitalize** all words of four letters or more - including prepositions and conjunctions. So *With*, *From*, *Over*, and *Between* are all capitalized in AP because they hit the four-letter threshold.
- **Capitalize** the first and last word of the title no matter what.
- **Lowercase** articles (*a, an, the*) and conjunctions and prepositions of **three letters or fewer** (*and, but, or, for, nor, the, a, an, to, of, in, on*) - unless they're first or last.

AP's four-letter rule is the easy shortcut to remember: if a minor word is four letters or longer, it gets a capital. Example in AP: **"A Guide to Cooking With Cast Iron Over an Open Fire."** Note *With* and *Over* are capitalized (four-plus letters), while *to*, *an*, and *a* stay lowercase.

## How to capitalize a title in Chicago style

Chicago style is the standard for books, academic publishing, and a lot of long-form content. It's stricter about parts of speech and ignores word length:

- **Capitalize** the first and last words, and all nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs.
- **Lowercase** all articles (*a, an, the*), coordinating conjunctions (*and, but, or, for, nor, so, yet*), and **all prepositions regardless of length** - including long ones like *between*, *throughout*, and *underneath*.
- **Capitalize** both parts of a hyphenated compound in most cases ("Self-Reflection," "Twentieth-Century Art").

The same example in Chicago: **"A Guide to Cooking with Cast Iron over an Open Fire."** Here *with* and *over* stay **lowercase** because Chicago treats all prepositions as minor words no matter how long they are. That single preposition rule is the biggest practical difference between the two styles.

## Which title case style should I use, AP or Chicago?

A quick way to decide:

- **Use AP** for press releases, news articles, blog posts, social copy, and most business and marketing writing. It's the dominant style for digital and journalistic content, and its four-letter rule is faster to apply by hand.
- **Use Chicago** for books, dissertations, academic papers, and formal long-form publishing - or whenever a publisher or professor specifies it.
- **Pick one and stay consistent.** Within a single site, document, or publication, mixing styles looks sloppy. Consistency matters more than which guide you chose.

If you're writing for the web with no house style, AP is the safe default. If you're submitting to a journal or publisher, check their guidelines first - many specify Chicago or APA.
