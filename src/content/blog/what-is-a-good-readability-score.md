"Make it more readable" is easy advice to give and hard to act on. A readability score turns that vague note into a number you can actually aim for. But the scores only help if you know what they mean - and what a *good* one looks like for the kind of writing you do. Here's a plain-English guide.

## What is a good readability score?

There's no single magic number, because "good" depends on your audience. The most useful rule of thumb comes from the Flesch Reading Ease scale, which runs from 0 (extremely hard) to 100 (very easy):

- **60-70** is considered the sweet spot for general web content, marketing copy, and blog posts. It's comfortably readable by most adults.
- **70-80** reads as conversational and easy - good for newsletters and consumer-facing pages.
- **30-50** is "college level" - fine for technical, legal, or academic writing where the reader expects density.
- **Below 30** is genuinely hard to read and usually a sign to break sentences up.

## What is a good Flesch Reading Ease score?

For most non-specialist writing, **aim for 60 or higher.** Major publications like newspapers tend to land in the 60s. A score in the 60-70 band means a typical 13-to-15-year-old can read your text without re-reading sentences - which, counterintuitively, is also where most adults prefer to read, because easy text is faster to skim.

A higher Flesch score is *easier*, not "better" in every context. Cranking it to 90 by writing only in tiny sentences can feel choppy and patronising. The goal is clarity for your specific reader, not the highest possible number.

## Is a higher or lower Flesch score better?

It depends which scale you're looking at, and this trips people up constantly:

- **Flesch Reading Ease:** higher is easier. 70 is easier to read than 40.
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level:** lower is easier. Grade 7 is easier than grade 12.

They're built from the same two ingredients (sentence length and syllables per word) but point in opposite directions. So "I want a higher score" only makes sense once you say *which* score.

## What is the Flesch-Kincaid grade level?

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level converts your text into a US school grade. A result of 8.0 means an eighth-grader (around age 13) should understand it. The formula rewards short sentences and short words: every long sentence or multi-syllable word nudges the grade up.

The difference between Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid is mostly presentation. Reading Ease gives you a 0-100 friendliness score; Flesch-Kincaid gives you a school grade. Same inputs, two ways of expressing the result.

## What does a grade 8 reading level mean?

A grade 8 reading level means the average 13-to-14-year-old could read the text comfortably. It is not an insult to your audience - it's the standard most readability guides recommend for general web and business writing, including health and government communication. Smart adults read grade-8 text faster precisely because it's frictionless.

## How is the Gunning Fog index calculated?

The Gunning Fog index estimates the years of formal education needed to understand a passage on the first read. It's based on two things:

1. **Average sentence length** (words divided by sentences).
2. **Percentage of "complex" words** - words of three or more syllables, excluding common suffixes and proper nouns.

Add those, multiply by 0.4, and you get the Fog index. A score of 12 means a high-school senior could follow it; under 10 is broadly accessible. Because it specifically penalises long words, Fog is good at catching jargon-heavy writing that other formulas miss.

## How many syllables make a word complex?

Across the common formulas, a word counts as "complex" or "hard" at **three or more syllables** (Gunning Fog uses this threshold). Flesch and Flesch-Kincaid don't use a hard cutoff - they count total syllables per word - but the practical takeaway is the same: long words drag every score down. Swapping *utilise* for *use*, *approximately* for *about*, or *demonstrate* for *show* lifts readability noticeably.

## Why is my readability score low?

Almost always, it comes down to two culprits:

- **Long sentences.** Anything over ~25 words starts to hurt. Sentences stacked with commas and "which" clauses are the usual offenders.
- **Long words.** Heavy abstract nouns (*implementation*, *optimisation*, *functionality*) and jargon spike the syllable count.

A low score isn't a character flaw - it's just a signal that the reader has to work harder than necessary.

## How do I make my writing easier to read?

A readability tool scores your text; it doesn't rewrite it for you, and that's deliberate - the edits are where your judgement matters. Here's what actually moves the number:

1. **Split long sentences.** One idea per sentence. Find the conjunction and make a full stop.
2. **Choose shorter words** where they mean the same thing.
3. **Cut filler** - "in order to", "the fact that", "it is important to note that".
4. **Fix the hardest sentences first.** A scorer that highlights your toughest sentences tells you exactly where to start.
5. **Read it aloud.** If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long.
